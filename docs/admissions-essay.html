Writing an essay that sounds like you

The essay is the one part of the application where the person behind the numbers speaks. Its whole job is to sound like a real, specific human — you — which is exactly why the clichés fail and honesty wins.

What the essay is for

The personal statement's job is not to list your achievements — the rest of the application already does that. Its job is to reveal how you think and what you value: to let a reader, after 650 words, feel they've met a specific person. The best essays are small and true, not grand and generic. A reader finishing thousands of these can tell instantly whether a voice is real. So the target isn't "impressive"; it's authentic and specific.

Finding a real topic

The topic matters less than the honesty. A great essay can be about something small — a habit, an object, a recurring moment, a change of mind — as long as it opens a genuine window into you. Prompts to mine:

  • A moment you changed your mind about something, and what that revealed.
  • A small thing you do or love that most people don't know about you.
  • A challenge you actually faced and what you learned — without forcing a tidy triumph.
  • A question or idea you can't stop thinking about, and why.

The test for a topic: could only you have written this? If someone else could swap their name in, it's too generic. Pick the story that's unmistakably yours.

Structure that works

You don't need a formula, but a reliable shape helps: open in a specific moment (a scene, not a thesis), reflect on what it means (this is the real content — your thinking), and show growth or insight without a clichéd bow. Two-thirds of a strong essay is usually reflection, not narration: the events are the excuse to show how you make sense of things. Start in the middle of a moment rather than with throat-clearing ("Ever since I was young…"), and end on a genuine thought rather than a moral.

The ratio to remember: show a real moment, then spend most of the words on what it made you think and who it made you. Admissions readers care far more about your reflection than the plot.

Voice and specifics

Two things make an essay feel human. Voice: write the way you actually talk (a little more careful), not in a stiff "essay" register or a thesaurus costume. Specifics: concrete, particular details — the exact object, the real quote, the specific smell — beat abstractions every time. "I love helping people" is empty; the specific afternoon you spent untangling a younger kid's maths, and what you noticed, is alive. Trade every generality for a detail and the essay comes to life.

Clichés to avoid

Readers see these thousands of times; they don't disqualify you, but they waste your chance to stand out:

  • The winning-the-big-game or the injury-that-taught-perseverance arc, told generically.
  • The mission-trip epiphany that centres you rather than the people you met.
  • The "and that's when I realised…" moral bolted onto the end.
  • A list of accomplishments dressed up as a narrative.
  • Big abstract words about passion, journey, and impact with no specific scene underneath.

The cure for all of them is the same: get specific and get honest. A true, small story always beats a grand, hollow one.

Supplements & "why us"

School-specific supplements — especially "why this college?" — test fit. Generic praise ("your prestigious university") is transparent. Instead, name specific, real reasons: a program, a professor's work, a way of learning, something concrete you'd do there — and connect it to who you are. Do a little genuine research per school; a few specific, personal sentences beat paragraphs of flattery. Reusing one supplement across schools without changing the specifics is the classic tell.

A sane process

  • Start early — good essays need drafts and distance, not a panicked night.
  • Write badly first. Get a true story down, then cut and shape. The first draft is for finding what you mean.
  • Read it aloud. If it doesn't sound like you talking, revise until it does.
  • Get one or two trusted readers — a teacher, a mentor — for honesty, not to sand off your voice. Feedback should sharpen you, not replace you.
  • Keep it yours. An essay written for you by someone else (or by a chatbot) reads flat and defeats the entire purpose — the point is your voice.
