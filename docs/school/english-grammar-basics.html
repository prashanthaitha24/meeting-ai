Grammar essentials: building a clear sentence

Grammar isn't a set of arbitrary rules to trip you up — it's the shared system that lets your writing be understood exactly as you mean it. You already use most of it without thinking. This lesson names the key parts so you can spot mistakes, fix them, and write sentences that are clear and correct.

The building blocks (parts of speech)

Every word in a sentence does a job. A noun names a thing (dog, city, freedom). A verb is an action or a state of being (run, is, think). An adjective describes a noun (a red car), and an adverb describes a verb, often telling how (she ran quickly).

A few more do the connecting work: pronouns stand in for nouns (he, she, it, they), prepositions show position or relationship (in, on, under, before), and conjunctions join things (and, but, because). You don't need to label every word — but knowing these lets you talk about, and fix, what a sentence is doing.

What makes a sentence

A complete sentence needs two things: a subject (who or what the sentence is about) and a verb (what they do or are). 'The dog barked' is a sentence — subject *dog*, verb *barked*. 'The dog in the yard' is not a sentence, because there's no verb telling us what the dog did.

A group of words missing a subject or a verb is a fragment, and it usually reads as incomplete. When you check your writing, make sure each sentence has both a subject and a verb and expresses a full thought.

Subject–verb agreement

The verb has to match the subject in number. A singular subject takes a singular verb (The dog runs); a plural subject takes a plural verb (The dogs run). This trips people up when words come between the subject and the verb — the verb still matches the real subject, not the nearest noun.

In 'The box of old photos is heavy', the subject is *box* (singular), so it's *is*, not *are* — even though *photos* sits right next to the verb. Find the true subject first, then match.

Worked example
Choose the correct verb: 'The list of names (was / were) on the desk.'
  1. Find the real subject — what the sentence is about. It's 'list', not 'names'.
  2. 'List' is singular, so it needs a singular verb.
  3. The singular form here is 'was'.
Answer: The list of names was on the desk.

Punctuation that matters most

A few punctuation rules fix most errors. Use a full stop to end a complete sentence. Don't join two full sentences with only a comma — that's a *comma splice* ('It was late, I went home'). Instead use a full stop, a semicolon, or a comma plus a joining word ('It was late, so I went home').

The apostrophe does two jobs: it shows possession (the dog's bowl) and marks missing letters in a contraction (do not → don't). A classic mix-up is *its* (belonging to it) versus *it's* (it is) — 'its' has no apostrophe when it means belonging, just like *his* and *hers*.

Check yourself

Try these. Pick an answer to see whether it's right and why.

In 'She sang beautifully', what part of speech is 'beautifully'?
'Beautifully' describes how she sang (the verb), which makes it an adverb. Adverbs often end in -ly.
Which of these is a complete sentence?
'Birds sing' has a subject (birds) and a verb (sing) and expresses a full thought. The others are fragments — no complete idea.
Choose the correct verb: 'The group of students ___ ready.'
The subject is 'group' (singular), not 'students', so it takes the singular verb 'is'.
Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
Two full ideas joined by a comma plus the conjunction 'so' is correct. A comma alone (first option) is a comma splice.
Which uses the apostrophe correctly?
'Its' (no apostrophe) shows belonging; 'it's' means 'it is'. The tail belongs to the dog, so 'its tail'.

In a nutshell

  • Words do jobs: nouns name, verbs act, adjectives describe nouns, adverbs describe verbs; pronouns, prepositions and conjunctions connect.
  • A complete sentence needs a subject and a verb and a full thought — otherwise it's a fragment.
  • Match the verb to the real subject, even when other words sit in between (the box … is).
  • Don't join two sentences with only a comma; and 'its' shows belonging while 'it's' means 'it is'.
