Multiplication: fast repeated adding

Multiplication is a shortcut for adding the same number many times. Instead of adding 4 + 4 + 4 slowly, you can say '3 times 4' and get the answer faster. Once you see multiplication as repeated adding — and as rows and columns — the times tables stop being something to fear and start to make sense.

Times means groups of

The times sign × means 'groups of'. So 3 × 4 means 'three groups of four', which is 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. Multiplication is just repeated addition written the short way, and it saves a lot of time when the groups are big.

A clear way to picture it is an array — rows and columns of dots. Three rows of four dots is 3 × 4, and you can count 12 dots altogether.

Order doesn't matter

Just like adding, multiplication can be done in any order and gives the same answer. 3 × 4 and 4 × 3 both equal 12 — three rows of four has the same number of dots as four rows of three, just turned on its side.

This is really useful: it halves how many facts you need to learn, because knowing 6 × 7 also gives you 7 × 6. It also lets you multiply in whichever order is easier for you.

Worked example
Work out 5 × 3.
  1. 5 × 3 means five groups of three (or three groups of five).
  2. As repeated adding: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3.
  3. Count up: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15.
Answer: 5 × 3 = 15

Learning the tables, and handy tricks

The times tables are just the answers to these facts, and knowing them by heart makes all later maths faster. A few tricks help: multiplying by 10 just adds a zero (7 × 10 = 70); multiplying by 2 is doubling; multiplying by 5 gives answers that always end in 0 or 5; and anything times 1 stays the same, while anything times 0 is 0.

Practise a little each day and the facts stick. They are the toolkit you'll reach for in division, fractions, area, and almost everything ahead.
