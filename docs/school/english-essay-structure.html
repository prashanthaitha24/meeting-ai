Structuring an essay that convinces

A good essay isn't about big words — it's about a clear idea, laid out so a reader can follow your thinking and end up agreeing. Most essays that lose marks aren't wrong; they're disorganised. This lesson gives you a reliable structure for the whole essay and for each paragraph, so your argument lands.

The shape of an essay

Almost every essay has three parts. The introduction tells the reader what the essay will argue — its main claim, called the thesis. The body is a series of paragraphs, each making one point that supports the thesis, with evidence. The conclusion pulls it together and restates the argument in light of what you've shown.

The golden rule: one main idea per paragraph. If a paragraph is trying to do two jobs, split it. A reader should be able to sum up each paragraph in a sentence — if they can't, it needs tightening.

The thesis: say what you're arguing

The thesis is the single sentence that states your position. 'Homework has both benefits and drawbacks' is weak because it doesn't take a side. 'Schools should reduce homework because it harms wellbeing without improving learning' is a real thesis — it makes a claim you can defend.

Everything in the essay should support that one claim. If a paragraph doesn't help prove the thesis, it doesn't belong, no matter how interesting it is. A clear thesis is the spine the whole essay hangs on.

Building a body paragraph (Point, Evidence, Explain)

A strong body paragraph follows a simple pattern. Point: open with a *topic sentence* stating the one idea of this paragraph. Evidence: give a fact, quotation, example or reason that backs it up. Explain: say *how* that evidence supports your point and links back to the thesis. Some teachers call this PEEL (Point, Evidence, Explain, Link).

The step students skip is the explain — they drop in a quote and move on. But evidence doesn't speak for itself; your job is to show what it proves. That explanation is where the real thinking, and the marks, are.

Worked example
Write a body paragraph arguing that libraries help communities.
  1. Point: 'Public libraries give everyone free access to information, regardless of income.'
  2. Evidence: 'A library card costs nothing, yet it opens up thousands of books, computers, and internet access.'
  3. Explain: 'This matters because a student without a computer at home can still research and learn — the library removes a barrier that money would otherwise create, supporting the whole community.'
Answer: Point → Evidence → Explain, all pointing back to 'libraries help communities'.

Introductions, conclusions, and flow

Open with a sentence that draws the reader in and narrows to your thesis by the end of the first paragraph — don't bury the thesis. The conclusion should not introduce new points; it restates your argument and leaves the reader with why it matters. Never just repeat the introduction word for word.

Between paragraphs, use linking words (however, in addition, as a result, on the other hand) so the reader feels the argument moving forward rather than jumping. Good flow is what makes separate points feel like one coherent case.

Check yourself

Try these. Pick an answer to see whether it's right and why.

What is a thesis?
The thesis is the one sentence stating the position the whole essay sets out to defend.
Which is the strongest thesis?
It takes a clear, defendable position and gives a reason — unlike the others, which state a topic or sit on the fence.
What should a topic sentence do?
A topic sentence opens the paragraph by stating its single main idea, which the rest of the paragraph then supports.
In the Point–Evidence–Explain pattern, which step do students most often skip?
Writers often give evidence and stop, forgetting to explain how it supports their point — which is where the real analysis and marks are.
What should a conclusion do?
A conclusion pulls the argument together and shows its significance; it shouldn't add new points or simply copy the introduction.

In a nutshell

  • An essay has an introduction (with a thesis), a body of one-idea paragraphs, and a conclusion.
  • The thesis is a single sentence taking a clear, defendable position; everything must support it.
  • Build each body paragraph as Point → Evidence → Explain — and never skip the explain.
  • Don't bury the thesis or add new points in the conclusion; use linking words so the argument flows.
