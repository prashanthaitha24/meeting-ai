SAT Reading & Writing: what's actually tested

The Reading & Writing section looks varied, but it's built from four repeating question types with predictable patterns. Learn the patterns and each question turns from a surprise into a routine you can drill.

The format in brief

Reading & Writing is the first section of the Digital SAT: two modules of 27 questions, 32 minutes each (54 questions, 64 minutes). Every question is a short, self-contained passage — usually 25 to 150 words — with a single multiple-choice question. There are no long passages with many questions any more. Questions are loosely grouped by type and ordered roughly easy-to-hard within a module. The College Board organises them into four content areas; below is what each actually asks and how to attack it.

Craft & Structure

These test how a text is built and what its words are doing.

Words in context

A passage has a blank (or an underlined word) and you pick the word that best fits. The trick: predict your own word first from the sentence's logic, then choose the option closest to it — don't read the four options cold, because two will often "sound" fine. Watch for contrast signals (although, but, however) that flip the meaning you need.

Text structure & purpose

You're asked the main purpose of a text or the function of a sentence within it. Ask "why did the author write this?" in your own words before looking at the choices. Wrong answers are usually true statements that aren't the purpose, or too broad or too narrow.

Cross-text connections

Two short texts on one topic; you identify how the second relates to the first — agreeing, qualifying, or disagreeing. Nail down each author's stance in a word before comparing.

Information & Ideas

These test comprehension and reasoning from the text (and sometimes a small graph or table).

Central ideas & details

Identify the main idea or a specific detail. The answer must be supported by the text alone — not what's generally true, not what you already know. If you can't point to the words that prove it, it's wrong.

Command of evidence

Two flavours. Textual: which quotation best supports a given claim. Quantitative: a short passage with a graph or table, and you pick the data point that supports (or completes) the argument. Read the axis labels and units carefully — most misses here are misreading the figure, not the logic.

Inferences

Complete a passage with the most logical conclusion. The right answer follows necessarily from what's stated; anything that requires an extra assumption is a trap.

The golden rule for this whole area: the answer lives in the passage. Every correct choice can be justified by pointing at specific words. If you're choosing based on outside knowledge or a "feeling," re-read.

Standard English Conventions

These are grammar and punctuation — the most learnable points on the test, because the rules are finite and repeat.

  • Joining clauses. Two independent clauses need a period, a semicolon, or a comma + conjunction — never a bare comma (a "comma splice"). This single rule is tested constantly.
  • Subject–verb agreement. Find the real subject, ignoring phrases in between ("The box of photos is…", not "are").
  • Punctuation pairs. Nonessential information is set off by a matching pair of commas (or dashes). Mismatched or single punctuation is usually wrong.
  • Pronouns and modifiers. A pronoun needs a clear noun; an opening phrase must modify the subject that follows it.

Because conventions rules are limited, they're the fastest points to gain: drill them until each is automatic. Our practice engine's Conventions topic targets exactly these.

Expression of Ideas

These test effective writing choices.

Transitions

Pick the transition word that fits the logic between two sentences — contrast (however), cause (therefore), addition (moreover), example (for instance). Describe the relationship yourself first, then match; don't audition each word.

Rhetorical synthesis

You're given bullet-point notes and a stated goal ("emphasise a difference," "introduce the topic"), and you choose the sentence that best meets that specific goal using the notes. Read the goal twice — the right answer is the one that does the stated job, even if another sounds nicer.

Tactics that cut across all of it

  • Predict before you peek. For nearly every question type, forming your own answer first stops the plausible-sounding wrong options from swaying you.
  • Prove it from the text. If you can't point to the words, don't pick it.
  • Watch the signal words. Although, but, however, because, therefore control the meaning — miss one and you pick the opposite.
  • Use the tools. The digital test lets you eliminate choices and flag questions — cross out wrong answers and move on; come back to the flagged ones.
  • Keep pace. About 71 seconds per question. If one is a puzzle, flag it and move — a hard question is worth the same as an easy one.
