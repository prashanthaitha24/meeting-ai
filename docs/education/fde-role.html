What a forward deployed engineer does

The role, where it came from, how it differs from software, solutions and support engineering, and what a real week looks like.

Beginner ~35 min read Module 1 of 20
Lesson Practice Cheat sheet Quiz

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is a software engineer whose workplace is the customer's problem. Instead of building one product for thousands of users from a distance, an FDE embeds with a specific customer, learns how their business actually works, and ships software that runs in their environment and changes a number they care about. The role was popularised by data-platform companies and is now common at AI companies, where every serious deployment needs someone who can bridge a powerful general product and a messy specific reality. This lesson explains what the job is, how it differs from neighbouring roles, the skills it demands, and what the work looks like week to week.

After this module you can
  • Describe the forward deployed engineer role and the problem it exists to solve
  • Distinguish FDEs from software, solutions, sales, support and consulting roles
  • Map the lifecycle of an FDE engagement from discovery to handoff
  • Name the technical and customer-facing skills the role requires
  • Assess whether the role fits your strengths and how to start building toward it

The problem FDEs solve

General-purpose software, especially AI, is powerful but rarely useful out of the box for a specific organisation. The customer's data lives in a decades-old ERP, a data lake nobody fully understands and a shared drive of PDFs. Their workflows have exceptions the vendor never imagined. Their network blocks outbound traffic, their security team wants a review, and the person who asked for the project is not the person who will use it. A product team far away cannot see any of this.

The forward deployed engineer closes that gap. They are "forward" because they work at the front line, often physically or virtually inside the customer's team, and "deployed" because the measure of success is software running in production for that customer, not a slide deck. An FDE takes a fuzzy goal such as "use AI to speed up claims handling", finds the part worth doing first, builds it with the customer's real data and systems, gets it through their security and operations processes, and proves it moved a metric.

Note

Titles vary: forward deployed engineer, forward deployed software engineer, deployment strategist, field engineer, AI deployment engineer. Read the job description, not the title: the signal is shipping code for specific customers in their environments.

The closest neighbour is the solutions engineer, and the line is mostly about depth and time: solutions engineers win the deal with demos and proofs of concept; FDEs typically arrive after or during the deal and stay until the software is in production and delivering value. Compared with consultants, FDEs usually represent a product company, so part of their job is feeding what they learn back into the product rather than building a bespoke system for every client.

The engagement lifecycle

  1. Discovery. Understand the business, the users, the data and the systems; find where software can change an outcome.
  2. Scoping. Agree a timeboxed pilot with explicit success criteria, owners and risks.
  3. Build and integrate. Connect to the customer's data and systems; build the application, often an AI workflow; iterate with real users weekly.
  4. Deploy. Package and install into the customer's environment, pass security review, set up monitoring.
  5. Measure. Compare the agreed metric before and after; collect evidence and user feedback.
  6. Handoff and expand. Train the customer team, document and transfer ownership, and identify the next use case; feed reusable work back into the product.
An engagement one-pager you keep updated from day one
text
Customer:       Acme Insurance, claims operations
Sponsor:        VP Claims (decides)      Users: 40 claims handlers
Problem:        Handlers spend ~25 min per claim finding policy clauses
Metric:         median handling time per claim (baseline: measure in week 1)
Pilot scope:    clause lookup assistant for motor claims only, 6 weeks
Data/systems:   policy PDFs (SharePoint), claims DB (read-only view)
Success:        -30% median handling time for pilot users, no accuracy loss
Risks:          data access approval; security review; handler adoption
Next decision:  week 3 go/no-go with sponsor

Each stage of this track maps to part of that lifecycle. The lifecycle is not strictly linear: a security review can send you back to redesign the integration, and a demo can reveal that discovery missed the real user. Good FDEs expect the loops and plan for them.

The skill profile

FDE work is often described as T-shaped: broad competence across the stack, with depth in one or two areas. Breadth matters because on any given day you might be debugging a TLS error in a customer's proxy, writing SQL against an unfamiliar schema, tuning a retrieval pipeline, and presenting to a vice president.

  • Software engineering: clean, tested code in at least one language (Python is the common denominator), APIs, data structures, version control.
  • Systems and DevOps: Linux, networking, containers, Kubernetes, CI/CD, enough cloud to deploy and debug anywhere.
  • Data: SQL, data modelling, pipelines, working with messy real-world data.
  • AI engineering (increasingly central): LLM applications, retrieval, agents, evaluation, cost and latency trade-offs.
  • Customer skills: discovery, scoping, written and spoken communication, managing expectations, handling conflict.
  • Judgement: choosing the smallest thing that proves value, knowing when to push back, and owning outcomes rather than tasks.
Tip

The most underrated FDE skill is writing. A clear one-page summary of what you learned in discovery, what you propose and what you need from the customer will unblock more projects than any amount of clever code.

What a week looks like

A representative week mid-engagement: Monday, a working session with the customer's operations team watching how they handle cases today, followed by turning notes into user stories. Tuesday and Wednesday, heads-down building: an integration with their document store, an evaluation set from twenty real cases, a fix for a bad retrieval result. Thursday, a security call to answer questions about data flows and a deployment to their staging cluster that fails on a proxy setting you then debug. Friday, a demo to the sponsor with real data, a written status update with risks and asks, and a note to your product team about a feature three customers have now requested.

The rhythm is high-context and interrupt-driven. You will context-switch more than a product engineer and own more ambiguity. In exchange you see the direct effect of your work on real people, and you learn more about how organisations really function in a year than many engineers learn in five.

Is it for you, and how to start

  • You enjoy talking to non-engineers and find their problems interesting, not a distraction from coding.
  • You are comfortable shipping something imperfect that works, then improving it with feedback.
  • You can debug systems you did not build, with incomplete access, under some pressure.
  • You can travel or work flexible hours when a deployment needs it, and you are fine without a stable, quiet roadmap.

To build toward it: complete the projects in this track and publish them with clear write-ups; take on internal "customer" work in your current job, such as building tools for another team; practise explaining technical trade-offs to non-technical friends; and get hands-on with deployments on infrastructure you do not control. The career lesson at the end of this track covers the interview in detail.

Hands-on practice

Map a real engagement

  1. Pick an organisation you know well (an employer, a school, a family business) and one process in it that wastes time.
  2. Write a one-paragraph problem statement in the customer's words, not technical terms.
  3. List the stakeholders: who sponsors, who uses, who owns the data, who must approve security.
  4. List the systems and data involved and how you would get access to each.
  5. Sketch the six lifecycle stages for this engagement with one concrete deliverable per stage.
  6. Write the single metric that would prove success and how you would measure it before and after.
Cheat sheet

What a forward deployed engineer does — at a glance

Main things to focus on

  • FDEs ship working software inside specific customers' environments and own the outcome.
  • Closest neighbour: solutions engineer (pre-sale); FDEs stay through production and value.
  • Lifecycle: discovery, scoping, build and integrate, deploy, measure, handoff and expand.
  • T-shaped: broad across code, systems, data, AI; deep in one or two.
  • Customer skills and writing are as important as code.
  • Feed field learnings back into the product; avoid bespoke forks.

Common pitfalls

  • Treating the role as sales support and never shipping production code.
  • Building a bespoke system per customer that the product team cannot maintain.
  • Starting to code before understanding who the real user is.
  • Measuring activity (features built) instead of outcomes (metric moved).
  • Under-communicating risks until they become surprises.
