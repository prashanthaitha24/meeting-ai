Communicating like an FDE

Demos, status updates, design docs, managing expectations, saying no well, and escalating before it is too late.

Beginner to intermediate ~35 min read Module 8 of 20
Lesson Practice Cheat sheet Quiz

An FDE's code only matters if the customer understands it, trusts it and decides to use it, and that depends on communication as much as engineering. This lesson covers the communication that fills an FDE's week: demos that land, weekly status updates that surface risk early, short design documents that get decisions made, managing expectations and saying no without damaging the relationship, and escalating problems before they become crises.

After this module you can
  • Structure and run a demo that ends in a decision
  • Write a weekly status update that makes risks and asks impossible to miss
  • Use short design documents to get technical decisions agreed
  • Manage expectations and decline requests with clear trade-offs
  • Escalate early, with evidence and options, to the right person

Demos that end in a decision

A demo is not a feature tour. It is a short story with a purpose: remind the audience of the problem, show the software solving it on their real data, show the evidence that it works, and ask for something. Keep it to the minimum that makes the point, usually fifteen to twenty minutes plus discussion.

  1. Problem (1-2 minutes): the pain in their words and numbers, from discovery.
  2. Live walkthrough (8-10 minutes): one realistic case end to end, using a real (approved) example, ideally driven by a user rather than by you.
  3. Evidence (3-5 minutes): metric versus baseline, evaluation results, user quotes.
  4. Limits and risks (2 minutes): what it does not do yet, honestly.
  5. Ask (1 minute): the decision or help you need, with a date.
Tip

Rehearse with the exact data and environment, have a recorded backup in case the network or a dependency fails, and never demo something you have not run that morning. A demo that fails on stage costs more trust than one that is modest.

The weekly status update

Send a written update every week, to the same people, in the same format. Its main job is not to report activity but to make risks and asks visible early, while they are still cheap to fix. Lead with an honest overall status.

A status update that takes two minutes to read
text
Subject: Claims assistant pilot - week 3 status: AMBER

Summary: Working version live for 5 of 12 handlers; accuracy on track;
security review not yet scheduled, which puts the week-5 rollout at risk.

Done this week
- Clause lookup working on 1,800 motor policies; 96% accurate on 120 reviewed cases
- 5 handlers using it daily; median lookup time 40s (baseline 6 min)

Next week
- Onboard remaining 7 handlers; expand evaluation set to 200 cases

Risks
- Security review unscheduled (owner: InfoSec). If not booked by Fri, rollout slips 1+ week.

Asks
- Sponsor: please help us book the security review this week.
- Team lead: 2 hours of reviewer time on Tuesday.
  • Status colours mean something: green on track, amber at risk with a plan, red will miss without a decision. Do not stay green until the week before failure.
  • Every risk has an owner and a consequence; every ask has a person and a date.
  • Use numbers, not adjectives: "96% on 120 cases" rather than "accuracy is good".

Short design documents

When a technical choice affects the customer (where data is processed, how you integrate with their system, what they will have to operate), write a one-to-two-page design note rather than deciding in a meeting. Written options get better feedback, include people who could not attend, and leave a record of why a decision was made.

  • Context: the problem and constraints in a few sentences.
  • Options: two or three, each with benefits, costs, risks and what the customer must do.
  • Recommendation and why.
  • Decision needed: from whom, by when.
  • Afterwards, record the decision at the top and keep the document with the project.

Expectations and saying no

Customers excited by a working prototype will ask for more: another use case, another data source, a mobile app. Saying yes to everything wrecks the pilot; saying no flatly damages the relationship. Make trade-offs visible instead: "We can add home policies; it would push the decision meeting by two weeks, or we could swap out the export feature. Which do you prefer?" The sponsor decides, with the consequences in view.

Set expectations about AI behaviour early and concretely. Show examples of mistakes the system makes and how they are caught, explain what the evaluation measures and what it does not, and avoid language that implies certainty. Customers forgive known limitations; they do not forgive surprises.

Watch out

Never promise dates or features on behalf of your product team in a customer meeting. Say you will find out, then follow up in writing.

Escalating early

Escalation is not failure; late escalation is. Escalate when a blocker threatens a milestone and you cannot remove it yourself within a few days: access that is not arriving, a stakeholder who has gone silent, a technical limitation in your product, a scope dispute.

  1. State the problem and its impact on the plan in one or two sentences.
  2. Show what you have already tried.
  3. Offer options, each with its consequence, and your recommendation.
  4. Name the decision or help you need and the date it is needed by.
  5. Send it to the person who can actually decide, and keep your customer champion informed so it is never a surprise to them.

Escalate inside your own company too: product gaps, bugs blocking a customer, or a deal at risk. Your account team, product managers and engineering leads can only help with problems they know about.

Hands-on practice

Communication drills

  1. Script a 15-minute demo of any project you have built using the five-part structure, then record yourself delivering it.
  2. Write a week-3 status update for an imaginary pilot that is AMBER, with two risks and two asks with owners and dates.
  3. Write a one-page design note comparing two ways to integrate with a customer system, ending with a decision request.
  4. Write a reply to a sponsor asking for a big new feature mid-pilot that offers two trade-off options.
  5. Write an escalation message about data access that is two weeks late, following the five steps.
  6. Ask a friend outside tech to read the status update and tell you what the risks are; rewrite until they can.
Cheat sheet

Communicating like an FDE — at a glance

Main things to focus on

  • Demo = problem, live real case, evidence, limits, ask.
  • Weekly status in a fixed format; risks and asks are the point.
  • Numbers, not adjectives.
  • Write design notes with options for decisions that affect the customer.
  • Say no with trade-offs and let the sponsor choose.
  • Escalate early with evidence, options and a date.

Common pitfalls

  • Feature-tour demos with no problem statement and no ask.
  • Green status until the week before a missed deadline.
  • Deciding customer-affecting architecture verbally with no record.
  • Promising product features or dates you do not control.
  • Escalating only after a milestone has already been missed.
