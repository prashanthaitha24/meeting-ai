Measuring outcomes and ROI

Evals and business metrics, baselines, before-and-after comparisons and the ROI case that renews the contract.

Advanced ~40 min read Module 17 of 20
Lesson Practice Cheat sheet Quiz

A pilot ends with a decision, and decisions are made on evidence. FDEs who can show credibly that their work moved a business metric get renewals, expansions and references; those who show a demo and anecdotes get polite thanks. This lesson covers designing the comparison so the result is believable, the confounders that fool everyone, combining adoption, quality and business metrics, turning time saved into money with honest ranges, and writing the outcome report and case study the customer's leadership will actually read.

After this module you can
  • Choose a comparison design: before and after, control group, or staggered rollout
  • Recognise confounders such as seasonality, selection and novelty effects
  • Combine adoption, quality and business metrics into one honest picture
  • Build an ROI estimate with explicit assumptions and ranges
  • Write an outcome report that leads to a clear decision

Designing a believable comparison

DesignHowStrengthWeakness
Before / afterSame users, metric before vs during pilotSimple, no extra users neededAnything else that changed gets credited to you
Control groupPilot users vs similar non-pilot users, same periodRemoves shared trends (season, volume)Groups may differ; needs enough users
Staggered rolloutTeams start at different weeks; compare each team before and afterStrong evidence with no permanent control groupNeeds planning from the start
A/B by caseRandomly route some cases to the toolStrongest causal evidenceNot always acceptable to users or the business

Choose the design during scoping, not after the pilot, because control groups and baselines cannot be created retroactively. Where possible, use a control group or staggered rollout: they protect you against the most common objection, "maybe it was just a quiet month".

Confounders that fool everyone

  • Seasonality and volume: claims spike after storms, support tickets after releases; compare like periods or use a control group.
  • Selection: if enthusiastic volunteers form the pilot group, they may have been faster anyway; compare against their own baseline or choose pilot users randomly.
  • Novelty and attention: people often perform better simply because they are being watched or have something new; look at whether gains persist after the first weeks.
  • Mix shift: if the pilot handled easier cases, average times fall for that reason alone; compare within case types.
  • Process changes: a new policy, staffing change or system upgrade during the pilot; record them in a timeline.
Tip

Keep a simple event log during the pilot (holidays, staffing changes, outages, releases). When a number moves, you can explain it, and you can pre-empt the sceptic in the room.

Three kinds of evidence together

No single number convinces everyone. Present adoption (are people using it?), quality (is it right?) and business impact (did the outcome move?) together; each answers a different stakeholder's question, and a strong result on one with weakness on another is itself a finding.

Adoption tells you whether the result can scale: a big effect among three enthusiastic users is not the same as a modest effect across a whole team. Quality tells you whether speed came at the cost of correctness, the first thing a risk or compliance stakeholder will ask. Business impact is what the sponsor bought. If adoption is low, find out why before claiming success; if quality dipped, say so and show the fix.

Median handling time, pilot vs control, with a bootstrap interval
python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("handling_times.csv")          # columns: group, minutes
pilot = df.loc[df.group == "pilot", "minutes"].to_numpy()
control = df.loc[df.group == "control", "minutes"].to_numpy()

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
diffs = [np.median(rng.choice(pilot, pilot.size)) - np.median(rng.choice(control, control.size))
         for _ in range(5000)]
low, high = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"median pilot {np.median(pilot):.1f} min vs control {np.median(control):.1f} min")
print(f"difference 95% interval: {low:.1f} to {high:.1f} minutes")

Report the interval, not just the point estimate: "pilot handlers were 5 to 8 minutes faster per claim" is more honest and more persuasive than "7 minutes faster", and it tells the sponsor how sure you are. Median is usually better than mean for times, because a few very long cases distort averages.

From minutes to money

Leaders decide on value. Translate the measured effect into money with every assumption written down and agreed with finance, and show a range rather than one optimistic figure.

An ROI estimate with explicit assumptions (illustrative numbers)
text
Measured effect:   6 min saved per claim (interval 5-8)
Volume:            9,000 claims/month in scope           (from claims system)
Loaded cost:       $45 per handler hour                  (agreed with finance)

Value per month    = 6 x 9,000 / 60 x $45 = $40,500
Value per year     = $486,000   (range: $405,000 - $648,000)
Running cost/year  = $48,000    (licences, infrastructure, model usage)
One-off cost       = $60,000    (integration and rollout)
First-year net     = $378,000   (range: $297,000 - $540,000)
  • Time saved is not money saved unless it is redeployed: say what the time enables (more claims per handler, faster customer response, less overtime).
  • Include quality effects where measurable: fewer errors, fewer escalations, less rework.
  • Include all costs honestly; a sponsor who finds a hidden cost stops trusting the whole case.

The outcome report

  1. Decision requested at the top: expand to all motor claims teams from next quarter.
  2. Headline result in one sentence with the interval.
  3. How it was measured: design, period, users, confounders handled.
  4. Adoption, quality, impact with one chart each.
  5. ROI with assumptions and range.
  6. What did not work and what you learned.
  7. Plan and costs for the next phase, with risks.

With the customer's permission, turn the report into a short case study for your company: problem, approach, result, quote. It is one of the most valuable things an FDE produces for the business, and it becomes portfolio evidence for you.

Hands-on practice

Analyse a pilot like a sceptic would

  1. Create a synthetic dataset of handling times for a pilot group and a control group over eight weeks, including a seasonal spike and a novelty effect that fades.
  2. Compute a naive before/after result for the pilot group alone, then the pilot-versus-control result; explain the difference.
  3. Compute a bootstrap interval for the median difference with the code above.
  4. Build an ROI estimate with written assumptions and a low, central and high case.
  5. Write a one-page outcome report following the seven steps, including a section on what did not work.
  6. Ask a colleague to argue against your conclusion for ten minutes and add answers to their objections.
Cheat sheet

Measuring outcomes and ROI — at a glance

Main things to focus on

  • Choose the comparison design during scoping, not after.
  • Control groups or staggered rollouts beat simple before/after.
  • Watch for seasonality, selection, novelty, mix shift and process changes.
  • Show adoption, quality and impact together.
  • Report intervals and ranges, with explicit, agreed assumptions.
  • Lead the report with the decision requested.

Designs

before/afterSimple; vulnerable to trends
pilot vs control, same periodRemoves shared trends
staggered rolloutEach team is its own comparison
random routing (A/B)Strongest causal evidence

Statistics

median for durationsRobust to long tails
bootstrap intervalResample to estimate uncertainty
compare within case typesAvoid mix-shift illusions
check persistence after week 2-3Novelty effects fade

ROI

value = effect x volume x unit costCore formula
net = value - running - one-offInclude all costs
low / central / highRanges, not one number
assumptions agreed with financeCredibility

Report order

decision -> headline -> methodTop of the report
adoption, quality, impact chartsEvidence
ROI, lessons, next phaseClose

Common pitfalls

  • Deciding how to measure after the pilot, with no baseline or control.
  • Crediting the tool for a seasonal dip in volume.
  • Pilot groups of enthusiastic volunteers compared with everyone else.
  • One optimistic ROI figure with hidden assumptions.
  • Reports that bury the decision on page four.
