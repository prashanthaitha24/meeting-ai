The FDE career and interview

The interview loop, case and decomposition questions, a portfolio that proves you can do the job, and growing in the role.

Advanced ~40 min read Module 20 of 20
Lesson Practice Cheat sheet Quiz

Forward deployed engineering is one of the fastest-growing engineering roles, especially at AI companies, and its interviews look different from a standard software loop. Alongside coding, you will be asked to break down an ambiguous customer problem live, design a deployment into an environment you do not control, and show how you handle difficult people and unclear situations. This lesson describes the typical interview loop, works through a decomposition question step by step, shows how to prepare, explains what a portfolio that proves you can do the job looks like, and maps where the career leads.

After this module you can
  • Describe the common stages of an FDE interview loop and what each assesses
  • Answer a decomposition (case) question with a clear, structured approach
  • Prepare system design answers for customer deployments, including constraints
  • Tell behavioural stories that show ownership, judgement and customer skill
  • Build a portfolio and plan for growth in the role

The typical interview loop

StageWhat it testsHow to prepare
Practical codingCan you build working software quickly: APIs, data wrangling, debuggingTimed exercises on realistic tasks, not only algorithm puzzles
Decomposition / caseCan you turn an ambiguous business problem into a planPractise out loud with a structure
System designCan you design a deployment under real customer constraintsDesigns that include data, security, networking, operations
Customer scenario / behaviouralOwnership, communication, handling conflict and ambiguityPrepared stories with numbers and outcomes
Presentation or take-home (some companies)Can you build and explain something end to endA polished project and a clear write-up

Loops vary by company, and the balance shifts with the product: AI companies weight LLM application design and evaluation heavily; data-platform companies weight data modelling and integration. Read the job description closely and ask the recruiter what each round covers; that is normal and expected.

Decomposition: thinking out loud

A decomposition question gives you a broad problem, such as "A hospital network wants to use AI to reduce nurse documentation time. How would you approach it?", and watches how you think. There is no single right answer; interviewers look for structure, good questions, sensible prioritisation and awareness of constraints.

A structure for decomposition answers
text
1. Clarify     Who is the customer and user? What does success look like? Constraints?
              (Which units? Current minutes per shift on notes? EHR system? Privacy rules?)
2. Map        Current workflow: when nurses document, in which system, what takes longest.
3. Options    e.g. ambient note drafting, smart templates, auto-filling vitals from devices.
4. Prioritise Value x feasibility x risk -> pick a narrow first pilot
              (draft shift handover notes for one ward, nurse reviews and signs).
5. Data/tech  Data needed, integration with the EHR, where models run, privacy controls.
6. Measure    Baseline minutes on documentation, note quality audit, adoption.
7. Risks      Clinical safety, privacy, nurse trust, EHR vendor limits; mitigations.
8. Next       Pilot plan, stakeholders to involve, decision point.
  • Ask clarifying questions first, but not forever: two to four, then state assumptions and move on.
  • Narrow aggressively: a specific first pilot shows judgement better than a grand vision.
  • Name the metric and how you would get a baseline.
  • Bring up risks and the people who could block the project before the interviewer does.
  • Summarise at the end in three sentences.

System design for customer deployments

FDE design questions add constraints that standard design interviews often skip: the customer's identity provider, their network and proxies, data that cannot leave a region or the network at all, an existing system you must integrate with, an operations team with limited Kubernetes experience. Strong answers make the constraints explicit and design around them.

  • Start with requirements and constraints: users and scale, data sensitivity and residency, integration points, latency, availability, who operates it.
  • Draw the data flow with trust boundaries, as in the security-review lesson.
  • Choose where components run (your cloud, their tenancy, on-prem) and justify it.
  • Cover identity and access, observability, deployment and upgrades, failure modes.
  • For AI systems, cover retrieval and permissions, model choice and hosting, evaluation and guardrails, cost.
  • Finish with what you would build first and how you would phase the rest.

Behavioural stories

Prepare six to eight stories you can adapt, using the STAR shape (situation, task, action, result) with numbers in the result. FDE interviewers listen for ownership of outcomes, comfort with ambiguity, customer empathy and honest handling of failure.

  • A time you discovered the real problem was different from the request.
  • A time you delivered under a hard deadline with incomplete information.
  • A time you disagreed with a customer or stakeholder and how it resolved.
  • A time something you built failed in production and what you did.
  • A time you said no, or traded scope, to protect a delivery.
  • A time you turned one-off work into something reusable.
Tip

Internal customers count. If you have not worked with external customers, stories about building tools for another team, supporting a launch or debugging another group's system demonstrate the same skills.

Portfolio and growth

The strongest evidence is work that looks like the job. The three projects in this track are designed for that: a customer pilot with discovery, scoping, a working AI application, evaluation and an outcome report; a robust legacy integration; and an air-gapped deployment. Publish each with a README that tells the story the way you would to a customer, including what you would do next.

Careers branch in several directions. Many FDEs grow into senior and lead FDEs who own the biggest or most complex customers and shape the team's playbooks. Others move into engineering management of FDE teams, product management (they know customer needs better than almost anyone), solutions architecture, or core product engineering with unusually strong customer instincts. A notable number found companies, because years of seeing the same unsolved problems across customers is excellent startup research.

Hands-on practice

A mock FDE loop

  1. Answer the hospital documentation decomposition question out loud in 20 minutes using the eight-step structure, and record yourself.
  2. Design, on paper, a document-question-answering system for a bank whose data cannot leave its network, covering identity, data flow, model hosting, evaluation and operations.
  3. Write six STAR stories from the list, each with a number in the result.
  4. Do a 60-minute practical coding exercise: build an API that ingests a CSV, validates it and serves aggregates, with tests.
  5. Publish one project from this track with a customer-style README and a one-page write-up.
  6. Ask a friend to run the decomposition and behavioural rounds with you as the interviewer and give blunt feedback.
Cheat sheet

The FDE career and interview — at a glance

Main things to focus on

  • Loops combine practical coding, decomposition, constrained system design and customer scenarios.
  • Decomposition: clarify, map, options, prioritise, data/tech, measure, risks, next.
  • Narrow to a specific first pilot; name the metric and the baseline.
  • Design around customer constraints: identity, network, residency, operations.
  • STAR stories with numbers; internal customers count.
  • Portfolio that looks like the job: pilots, integrations, deployments with write-ups.

Common pitfalls

  • Jumping to a solution in a case question without clarifying.
  • Endless clarifying questions and no plan.
  • System designs that ignore the customer's network, identity and data rules.
  • Behavioural stories with no measurable result.
  • A portfolio of tutorials instead of end-to-end, customer-style projects.
