Handoff, enablement and long-term support

Runbooks, training, ownership transfer and support agreements so the customer succeeds after you leave.

Advanced ~35 min read Module 19 of 20
Lesson Practice Cheat sheet Quiz

An FDE engagement succeeds when the customer can run, use and improve what you built without you. Handoff is where many good projects quietly fail: the FDE moves to the next customer, knowledge leaves with them, the system drifts, and six months later nobody remembers how the integration works. This lesson covers planning handoff from the start, the documentation set that makes a system operable, enablement for users, operators and developers, defining ownership and support boundaries, and the follow-up that keeps the deployment healthy and growing.

After this module you can
  • Plan handoff from day one, with named customer owners for each area
  • Produce the documentation set: architecture, runbooks, configuration and decision records
  • Run enablement sessions for users, operators and developers with hands-on exercises
  • Define ownership, support tiers and escalation paths in writing
  • Measure handoff readiness and schedule follow-ups that sustain adoption

Plan handoff from the first week

Handoff is not a final-week activity. In the first week, ask who will own the system after you leave: who operates it, who supports users, who changes configuration, who decides on improvements. Involve those people throughout: let the future operator run the deployments with you, let the future admin change configuration while you watch. By the end, the handoff is a formality because they have already been doing the work.

The documentation set

  • Overview: what the system does, for whom, and the success metric it serves.
  • Architecture: the data-flow diagram from the security review, components, dependencies and where everything runs.
  • Configuration reference: every setting, its default, what changing it does, and who may change it.
  • Runbooks: step-by-step procedures for routine tasks and known failures.
  • Decision records: short notes on significant decisions and why they were made, so future maintainers do not undo them by accident.
  • Contacts and support: who owns what, how to reach your company, escalation paths.

Enablement by audience

Different people need different training. Keep sessions short, hands-on and recorded, and give every participant something to do, not just watch.

  • Users: a 30-minute session on doing their real work with the tool, what it is good and bad at, how to check its answers, and how to give feedback. Short guides and a few example tasks beat long manuals.
  • Champions and admins: configuration changes, access management, reading usage and quality dashboards, collecting feedback.
  • Operators: a game-day where they deploy, upgrade, roll back and fix two injected failures using only the runbooks while you observe silently.
  • Developers (if the customer will extend it): the codebase, the extension points, how to run tests and evaluations, and how to contribute.
Tip

The game-day is the most reliable readiness test. If operators cannot recover from a failure using the runbook while you sit on your hands, the runbook or the training is not finished.

Ownership and support boundaries

Write down who does what after go-live. A common model has three tiers: the customer's service desk handles first-line questions; the customer's platform team handles operations using runbooks; your company's support handles product defects and escalations under the support agreement. Define severity levels, response times, how to open a case, and what information to include (the support bundle). Ambiguity here produces either angry customers or an FDE who never really leaves.

Readiness and follow-up

  1. Readiness checklist signed off by each customer owner: docs reviewed, game-day passed, access transferred, monitoring and alerts routed to them.
  2. Access cleanup: remove your accounts and credentials, rotate any secrets you handled, confirm in writing.
  3. Data cleanup: delete any copies of customer data you held, as agreed in the access request.
  4. Follow-up cadence: check-ins at two weeks, six weeks and three months reviewing adoption, quality, incidents and the next use case.
  5. Retrospective with your own team: what to add to templates, playbooks and the product.

Follow-ups are not just courtesy. Adoption often drops after the FDE leaves; catching it early, with fresh training or a small fix, protects the outcome you worked for, and the three-month review is usually where the next engagement starts.

Hands-on practice

Hand over a system you built

  1. Pick one project from this track and write the full documentation set: overview, architecture, configuration reference, three runbooks, two decision records, contacts.
  2. Run a 30-minute user session for a friend acting as a user, with three real tasks.
  3. Run a game-day: a friend acting as operator must deploy, upgrade, roll back and fix two failures you inject, using only your runbooks.
  4. Improve every runbook step where they got stuck.
  5. Write a support model: tiers, severities, response times and how to open a case with a support bundle.
  6. Write the readiness checklist and a follow-up plan for two weeks, six weeks and three months.

Common pitfalls

  • Starting handoff in the final week.
  • Documentation that describes the code but not how to operate it.
  • Training by demonstration only, with no hands-on practice.
  • Alerts still routed to the FDE after go-live.
  • Leaving with active credentials and copies of customer data.
