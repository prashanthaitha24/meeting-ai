Scoping pilots and proofs of concept

Turning discovery into a timeboxed pilot: success criteria, assumptions, risks, a statement of work and a plan that survives contact.

Beginner to intermediate ~40 min read Module 6 of 20
Lesson Practice Cheat sheet Quiz

Discovery tells you what is worth doing; scoping decides exactly what you will do, by when, with whose help, and how everyone will know it worked. A good scope is the difference between a pilot that ends in a confident yes or no and one that drifts for months. This lesson covers choosing between a proof of concept, a pilot and a production project, writing success criteria that cannot be argued about later, drawing scope boundaries, listing the customer's own commitments, building a timeline with go/no-go gates, keeping a risk register, and handling change without losing control.

After this module you can
  • Choose between a proof of concept, a pilot and a production engagement
  • Write measurable success criteria and explicit in-scope and out-of-scope lists
  • Record assumptions and customer dependencies with owners and dates
  • Build a timeboxed plan with milestones and go/no-go gates
  • Maintain a risk register and a lightweight change-control process

Proof of concept, pilot or production?

TypeQuestion it answersTypical lengthReal users and data?
Proof of concept (POC)Can this work at all technically?1-3 weeksSample data, no real users
PilotDoes it deliver value in their real workflow?4-12 weeksReal data, a limited group of real users
Production rolloutCan it run for everyone, reliably and supported?MonthsEveryone, with SLAs

Match the engagement to the open question. If feasibility is genuinely uncertain (can we extract fields from these scanned forms at all?), run a short POC first. If feasibility is clear and the doubt is about value and adoption, go straight to a pilot with real users, because a POC on sample data proves nothing about the workflow. Many stalled AI projects are endless POCs: technically impressive, never touching a real user.

Success criteria and scope boundaries

Turn the discovery metric into criteria that are specific, measurable, owned and time-bound, and write down what you are explicitly not doing. An out-of-scope list is as important as the in-scope one: it is what you point to when someone asks for one more feature in week four.

A pilot plan as a reviewable document (fields are illustrative)
yaml
pilot: motor-claims-clause-assistant
duration_weeks: 6
users: 12 claims handlers in the Leeds team
success_criteria:
  primary: median handling time for pilot users down >= 30% vs 2-week baseline
  guardrails:
    - clause-lookup accuracy >= 95% on a 200-case expert-reviewed set
    - no increase in escalation rate
    - weekly active use >= 80% of pilot users by week 5
in_scope:
  - motor policies, English-language PDFs
  - read-only access to the claims view
  - web app behind company SSO
out_of_scope:
  - home and commercial policies
  - writing back to the claims system
  - mobile app
decision: go/no-go for wider rollout at end of week 6, sponsor decides
Tip

Agree what happens on a 'no' as well as a 'yes'. If the pilot misses its criteria, a clear stop with lessons learned is a good outcome; a vague extension is not.

Assumptions and customer dependencies

Most pilot delays are caused by things the customer must provide, not by engineering. List every dependency with an owner and a date, and treat late dependencies as schedule changes, visibly.

Also write down assumptions: things you believe but have not verified, such as "PDFs contain selectable text, not scanned images" or "the claims view refreshes nightly". Verify the riskiest ones in the first week; a wrong assumption found in week five is a crisis, found in week one it is a plan change.

A timeline with gates

  1. Week 0: kickoff. Confirm scope, success criteria, dependencies, communication rhythm and decision makers.
  2. Week 1: foundations. Access, data samples, baseline measurement, riskiest assumptions tested.
  3. Weeks 2-3: first working version with real data, shown to users; evaluation set built with experts.
  4. Gate (end of week 3): is it on track to meet criteria? Adjust, continue or stop.
  5. Weeks 4-5: users on it daily, weekly iterations, security approval, monitoring in place.
  6. Week 6: measure and decide. Compare against baseline, present evidence, sponsor decides go or no-go.

Estimate in ranges and say what drives the uncertainty ("2-4 weeks, depending on whether the forms are scanned"). When you cannot estimate at all, schedule a short spike, a timeboxed investigation whose only output is the information needed to estimate.

Risks and change control

Keep a short risk register from day one: each risk with likelihood, impact, mitigation and owner, reviewed in the weekly status. Typical FDE risks are data access, data quality, security approval, user adoption, scope creep and key-person availability on the customer side. Writing a risk down and sharing it is itself a mitigation: it turns a future surprise into a known, owned issue.

Change will come: a new stakeholder, a new use case, a discovery that the data is worse than expected. Handle it explicitly. Write the proposed change, its effect on timeline, criteria and effort, and get the sponsor's decision: swap it for something else in scope, extend, or defer it to the next phase. Saying "yes, and here is what moves" is how you stay helpful without losing the pilot.

Watch out

Silent scope creep is the most common way good pilots fail: each small addition is reasonable, together they make the deadline impossible, and the decision meeting arrives with nothing finished.

The statement of work

Commercial engagements wrap the plan in a statement of work (SOW) agreed between the companies. As an FDE you usually help draft its technical parts. Make sure it contains the objective, scope and out-of-scope lists, deliverables, success criteria, customer responsibilities, timeline and milestones, acceptance process, assumptions, and how changes are handled. Anything vague in the SOW becomes a disagreement later; anything technical that is promised must be something you know you can deliver.

Hands-on practice

Scope the pilot from your discovery

  1. Take the discovery summary from the previous lesson's practice and decide whether the next step is a POC or a pilot, with one sentence of justification.
  2. Write the pilot plan in the YAML structure above: users, duration, primary metric, guardrails, in-scope and out-of-scope lists, decision.
  3. List at least five customer dependencies with owners, dates and the impact of each being late.
  4. List three assumptions and how you would test each in week one.
  5. Draw a six-week timeline with a mid-point gate and a final decision meeting.
  6. Write a risk register with five risks and a change request for a plausible new feature, showing what would move.
Cheat sheet

Scoping pilots and proofs of concept — at a glance

Main things to focus on

  • POC for feasibility, pilot for value with real users, production for everyone.
  • Success criteria: specific, measured against a baseline, owned, dated.
  • Out-of-scope list is your protection against creep.
  • Customer dependencies cause most delays: owner and date for each.
  • Gates and a final decision meeting; agree what a 'no' looks like.
  • Every change gets a visible trade-off and a sponsor decision.

Common pitfalls

  • Endless POCs on sample data that never meet a real user.
  • Success criteria like 'users like it' that cannot be measured.
  • No out-of-scope list, so every request seems reasonable.
  • Treating late customer dependencies as your problem silently.
  • Extending a failing pilot instead of stopping with lessons learned.
