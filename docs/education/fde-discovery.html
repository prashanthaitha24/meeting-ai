Customer discovery

Framing the real problem: stakeholder maps, discovery interviews, current-state workflows and success metrics that matter to the business.

Beginner to intermediate ~40 min read Module 5 of 20
Lesson Practice Cheat sheet Quiz

The most expensive FDE mistake is building the right software for the wrong problem. Discovery is how you avoid it: a short, deliberate period of learning how the customer's business works, who actually feels the pain, what data and systems are involved, and what outcome would be worth paying for. This lesson gives you a working method: mapping stakeholders, running discovery interviews that surface facts instead of opinions, watching the real workflow, choosing success metrics with baselines, and writing it all into a one-page discovery summary the customer signs off.

After this module you can
  • Map stakeholders by role, influence and interest, and find the real user
  • Run discovery interviews with open, behavioural questions that avoid leading
  • Document the current-state workflow and where time, errors and cost go
  • Choose success metrics with a measurable baseline
  • Write a discovery summary that turns findings into a decision

Why discovery comes first

Requests arrive pre-solved: "we need a chatbot", "build us a dashboard", "connect AI to SharePoint". Behind each is a business problem the requester may not have articulated, and often several people with different views of it. Discovery replaces the solution in the request with a clear statement of the problem, the people, the constraints and the measure of success. It typically takes days to a couple of weeks, and it is the cheapest point in the project to change direction.

Discovery is not a formality before "real work". Its output directly drives scoping, data access requests, the security review, the evaluation set and the demo. FDEs who skip it spend the later stages rediscovering the same facts at much higher cost.

Map the stakeholders

RoleWhat they care aboutWhat you need from them
Executive sponsorBusiness outcome, cost, risk, timelinePriority, funding, decisions, air cover
ChampionMaking the project succeed (often their idea)Introductions, internal navigation, honest feedback
End usersTheir daily work getting easier, not harderWorkflow details, real examples, testing
Data and system ownersTheir systems' stability and data protectionAccess, schemas, rate limits, change windows
Security, legal, complianceRisk, data handling, regulationApprovals, requirements, review dates
IT and platform teamsSupportability, standardsInfrastructure, networking, deployment path

The sponsor who asked for the project and the users who will live with it are rarely the same people, and their goals can conflict: the sponsor wants handling time down, the users fear being measured. Talk to both early. Also identify blockers, people with the power to stop the project who are not yet involved, typically security, a works council or the owner of a critical system. Bringing them in early is far easier than winning them over after a surprise.

Discovery interviews that produce facts

People are poor at predicting what they would use and good at describing what they did. Ask about specific past behaviour, follow the numbers, and keep your solution out of the conversation until you understand the problem.

A question bank for a 45-minute user interview
text
Context      What does a normal day look like in your role?
Last time    Walk me through the last time you handled one of these, step by step.
Tools        Which systems and files did you open? Where did you copy things between them?
Time         Roughly how long did each part take? Which part took longest?
Exceptions   What makes a case hard? What happens when information is missing?
Quality      How do you know when it was done right? What mistakes happen?
Workarounds  What spreadsheets, notes or scripts have you built to cope?
Impact       If this part took half the time, what would you do with it?
Data         Can you show me a real (redacted) example?
Close        Who else should I talk to? What did I not ask that I should have?
  • Avoid leading questions such as "would an AI assistant help?"; everyone says yes and it tells you nothing.
  • Ask "why" a few times to move from symptom to cause, but stop before it feels like an interrogation.
  • Quantify: how many per day, how long each, how often wrong, what it costs when wrong.
  • Take notes in the person's own words; quotes are powerful later in the discovery summary and the ROI case.
  • Interview several users; one power user's workflow is rarely typical.

Watch the real workflow

Where you can, shadow users doing the work rather than only hearing about it. People skip steps they consider obvious, and the obvious steps are often where the time goes: copying an ID from one system to another, searching a shared drive, waiting for a colleague's answer. Draw the current-state workflow as a sequence of steps with the systems involved, the time spent and the decision points.

Mark three things on the map: where time accumulates (candidates for automation or assistance), where errors are introduced (candidates for validation), and where judgement is required (where software should support, not replace, the human). A good first project usually targets one high-volume step with clear inputs and outputs, not the entire workflow.

Success metrics and baselines

Agree what success means before building anything, in terms the sponsor already uses. A metric without a baseline measured before you start cannot prove anything afterwards.

LevelExample metricWho cares
Business outcomeCost per claim, revenue per rep, days to closeSponsor, finance
OperationalMedian handling time, backlog, first-contact resolutionTeam leads, users
QualityError rate, escalation rate, accuracy against expert reviewCompliance, users
AdoptionWeekly active users, share of cases using the toolChampion, product team
Technical guardrailsLatency, availability, cost per requestIT, your team
Tip

Pick one primary metric and two or three guardrails, such as "median handling time down 30% with no increase in error rate". A single number with guardrails keeps everyone honest; ten equally weighted metrics invite cherry-picking.

The discovery summary

End discovery with a document of one or two pages that the sponsor reads and agrees to. It is the contract between what you learned and what you will build, and it becomes the opening section of the scoping document.

  1. Problem statement in the customer's words, with numbers.
  2. Users and stakeholders, including who decides and who must approve.
  3. Current-state workflow with where time and errors accumulate.
  4. Data and systems involved, with owners and access status.
  5. Opportunities, ranked by value and feasibility, with a recommended first one.
  6. Success metric, baseline plan and guardrails.
  7. Constraints and risks: security, data, timeline, adoption.
  8. Decision requested: proceed to scoping the recommended pilot, by a date.
Hands-on practice

Run a discovery on a real process

  1. Choose a process at work, school or a local organisation that involves repetitive information handling.
  2. Draw the stakeholder table for it, including at least one potential blocker.
  3. Interview two people who do the work using the question bank; record specific past examples and numbers.
  4. Shadow one of them for 30 minutes if possible and draw the current-state workflow with time per step.
  5. Choose one primary metric and two guardrails, and describe how you would measure the baseline.
  6. Write a one-page discovery summary ending with a clear decision request, and ask one interviewee whether it reflects their reality.
Cheat sheet

Customer discovery — at a glance

Main things to focus on

  • Replace the solution in the request with a problem statement, people and a metric.
  • Sponsors and users differ; talk to both, and find blockers early.
  • Ask about specific past behaviour, not hypothetical future use.
  • Shadow the workflow; mark where time, errors and judgement are.
  • One primary metric with a baseline, plus guardrails.
  • End with a one-page summary and a decision request.

Common pitfalls

  • Accepting the requested solution without understanding the problem.
  • Interviewing only the sponsor and never the users.
  • Leading questions that confirm what you already planned to build.
  • No baseline, so success can never be demonstrated.
  • Discovering the security or data-owner blocker in week five.
