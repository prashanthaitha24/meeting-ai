The application, piece by piece

An application is a set of parts, each doing a specific job in building the picture of you. Knowing what each part is for — and the common mistakes on each — lets you make every piece pull its weight.

Grades & course rigour — the biggest factor

Your transcript — the courses you took and the grades you earned — is the single most important part, because it's years of evidence, not a single moment. Colleges read it for rigour (did you take the hardest courses available to you?) and performance (how did you do in them?). An upward trend helps. Common mistake: loading up on easy classes for a higher GPA. A strong grade in a demanding course beats a perfect grade in an easy one.

Test scores

Where required or submitted, the SAT or ACT is one academic data point among several. Many colleges are test-optional — always check each school's current policy, because it changes. Submit scores when they strengthen your application and are at or above a school's typical range; when they'd drag it down and the school is test-optional, you can withhold them. Common mistake: obsessing over a few points while neglecting essays and coursework that matter more. Prep efficiently (our free SAT practice and SAT guide help), then move on.

Activities list

A short, structured list of what you do outside class. Its job is to show depth, commitment, and impact — not to fill every line. Order it by what matters most to you, describe each with action and result ("built X used by Y people," "led a team of N"), and let a couple of genuine commitments carry it. Common mistake: padding with one-off memberships. A focused list showing real involvement beats a full one showing none.

The essays

The personal statement (the main essay) and school-specific supplements are where the human behind the numbers appears. The main essay's job is to reveal who you are — how you think, what you value — through a real, specific story, not to list achievements the rest of the application already shows. Supplements (especially "why us") show fit and that you've thought about the specific school. This is the part you most control late in the process; we cover it in depth in writing the essay. Common mistake: a generic essay that could have been written by anyone, or one that just restates the résumé.

Recommendations

Letters from teachers and a counsellor offer an outside view: how you show up in a classroom and community, your character and growth. Their job is credible, specific corroboration of your story. Choose teachers who know you well and have seen you engaged — ideally in a core subject and recent — over the most famous teacher who barely knows you. Common mistake: asking late, or picking a big name over someone who can write with real, specific detail. Ask early and politely, and give them what they need (a résumé, a note on what you'd love them to mention).

Interviews

Where offered, an interview (often with an alumnus) is usually informational and evaluative but rarely decisive. Its job is a two-way conversation: they learn about you, you learn about the school. Be yourself, be curious, and prepare a few genuine questions. Common mistake: treating it as an interrogation or reciting rehearsed lines — it's a conversation, and warmth and curiosity carry it.

Deadlines & logistics

The pieces above only count if they arrive, complete and on time. Know the platforms (the Common Application is used by many schools) and the deadline types:

OptionWhat it means
Early Decision (ED)Binding — you commit to attend if admitted. Apply ED only to a clear first choice.
Early Action (EA)Non-binding early deadline; you hear sooner but aren't committed.
Regular Decision (RD)The standard deadline, usually in winter, with decisions in spring.
RollingReviewed as they arrive; apply early for the best odds.

Common mistake: missing a supplement, a recommendation, or a financial-aid form because you tracked only the main deadline. Keep a checklist per school. And don't overlook financial aid deadlines — for many families the aid application matters as much as the admission one.
